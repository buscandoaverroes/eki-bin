# What v1.7 taught us

_A companion to the [v1.7 release notes](v1.7.md): those say what changed,
this says what was learned. The full record, dated as it happened, is in
[`docs/insights.md`](../insights.md); the numbers here come from it and from
[`docs/imu-interaction-plan.md`](../imu-interaction-plan.md)._

## 1. The glass decides the design

- **Clear glass ruins it.** The first test (June) put LEDs in a clear vase:
  you saw wires and bare LEDs, and the effect was gone. Thick brown glass
  hid the electronics and made the light look like it belonged to the
  bottle. It became the reference vessel.
- **Thick amber glass deletes a colour.** It's a blue-cut filter. It doesn't
  dull every hue evenly; it collapses them onto the red–green axis, so light
  blue reads yellow-green and violet reads red. Measured with the same
  test in three bottles: thick brown separates 2–3 line colours, mid brown
  4–5, clear brown 5 or more. Turning the brightness up can't help, because
  it scales the channels the glass hasn't removed.
- **It compresses brightness too.** Steps that are obvious in a clear bottle
  are barely visible in thick brown, so a setting tuned in one vessel doesn't
  transfer to another.
- **The most beautiful vessel is the least functional.** Thick, opaque glass
  gives the richest refraction, the triangle of light in the GIF, and it
  is the one capped at 2–3 lines.
- **And it isn't the bottle people bring.** Mentioning the project in
  passing, the first thing people often said was "I have a bottle I'd want
  you to make this in." Of about ten bottles offered that way, only two were
  the dark, thick kind the physics seemed to need. The rest were light
  green, light blue, nearly clear, and each had different physics. The wish
  to have it in *their* bottle turned out to matter more than holding to one
  hardware design.
- **That turned the project around:** from hiding LEDs behind the right
  glass to making the light itself visible in any glass, and dressing it to
  be seen. That's where the filament [chandelier](../chandelier-concept.md)
  comes from, and why per-bottle tuning stays out of the firmware.

## 2. Everything goes in through a 22 mm neck

The bottle was, literally, the bottleneck: every iteration had to fit
through its neck.

- **The neck chooses the parts.** A hobbyist can't order a custom PCB or
  chip; you build from what the parts shop has. Many of those boards don't
  fit. The NFC reader boards sold locally, for example, can't pass the
  neck, so the bottle limited the parts before the design did.
- **The opening is small.** A typical neck bore is 18–25 mm; this bottle's
  is 22 mm. A XIAO board alone is about 21 × 18 mm, so the microcontroller,
  clock, motion sensor and wiring only just pass, one at a time.
- **Assembly happens through the mouth.** Everything is wired up, fed in,
  then mounted. The motion sensor needs a firm mount 10–20 cm below the
  opening, where no finger reaches: about 20 minutes with a contraption of
  tape and chopsticks small enough to fit the mouth.
- **Cutting the bottle open isn't an option.** Taking off the base and
  rejoining it needs tools a hobbyist doesn't have, and even with a
  makerspace it would ruin the bottle, which is the point of the whole
  project.
- **Loose wires inside a bottle are a reliability problem.** The motion
  sensor resetting itself, the "asleep and can't be woken" bug, traced to
  the wiring: 12 resets in 11 minutes of handling, mostly with the bottle
  leaning one way, and 2 in 70 minutes at rest.
- **So the next build is assembled outside the vessel.** A lid or neck unit
  carries the electronics, and only the light goes into the glass.

## 3. A bottle in a hand is hard physics

- **Every bottle answers a tap differently.** Tap *position* (neck or
  body) was 98% separable within one bottle and 0% across two: worse than a
  guess. Tap *count* transferred (85%). So the project fixes behaviour
  (the state machine) before tuning thresholds, which belong to one bottle.
- **The first moment of a grab can look just like a tap.** What tells them
  apart arrives later: a finger leaves the bottle still, a hand doesn't. A
  hold-then-tilt starts rotating up to ~1.4 s after the grab, while a
  tap-then-grab can grab 1 s after the tap, so no fixed waiting time works.
  Stillness does (AUC 0.92).
- **Natural taps are gentle, and sideways.** Half of them never reached the
  classifier, because the trigger measured the *length* of the acceleration
  vector, and a sideways tap barely changes it. Earlier tests had used harder,
  performed taps.
- **The table is part of the system.** A knock on the table arrives
  vertically through the base. A sideways bump to the table looks like a tap
  at first, then sways for about three seconds. A hard tap can look like
  the start of a tilt about one time in five.
- **Measure, don't tune.** Each of these came from recordings: a raw 240 Hz
  recorder, a guided protocol where the script cues each trial so both hands
  move naturally, and a simulator that replays the real firmware. Tuning by
  feel had stopped converging.

## 4. Battery, or a lamp?

- **The LED strip draws power when it's dark.** Each WS2812B's driver chip
  runs whenever it has power: about 17 mA for 21 LEDs showing nothing. On
  three AA batteries that's 4 days. With a switch cutting the strip's power
  between uses and shorter wake periods, it's about 55 days.
- **For a bright display, wake-ups matter more than microamps.** Once the
  strip is switched off when idle, battery life depends mostly on how often
  it wakes, not on the board's sleep current.
- **A lamp dissolves the problem instead of solving it.** If the object is a
  light on a cord, powered from the top, there's no battery to manage, the
  electronics naturally live in the lid, and the board can afford to run
  sensing and models all the time. The power question and the input question
  trade off against each other, which is why the form isn't decided yet.

## 5. The light is the interface

- **Brightness depends on the room as much as the glass.** A day with the
  bottle found daylight too dim and night fine, the reverse of a reading
  through different glass ten weeks earlier. One brightness can't serve a
  room that swings by orders of magnitude, so it changes with the time of
  day, and tilt sets it by hand.
- **Smooth beats alive.** Six ways of moving a "train" and five fades were
  compared on real glass. The smooth ones won every time. The lively ones
  read as "something needs your attention", which is the opposite of
  ambient.
- **A tap that does nothing is the worst failure.** Silence reads as broken,
  so every tap gets an answer, even if that answer is "nothing to change to".
- **Dark is the default.** The bottle should be worth having on the table
  unlit. The light is an indicator that's there when you ask, which is also
  what makes the power budget possible.

## What this changes next

1. Switch on the rebuilt input pipeline once the rest recordings show under
   one false tap per hour.
2. Build the electronics as a lid unit, assembled outside the vessel.
3. Treat the form (bottle, lamp or chandelier) as the open question it is,
   and keep per-bottle numbers out of the firmware until it's settled.
